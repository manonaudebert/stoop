Key Tenant Questions
1. Does my landlord actually take care of the building?
People understand that issues happen in buildings. What they care about is:
* whether management responds
* whether issues get resolved quickly
* whether problems are recurring
* whether the building feels neglected
Prospective tenants use proxies to evaluate this:
* cleanliness of common spaces
* maintenance of hallways/elevators
* visible repairs
* online reviews
* responsiveness during the leasing process
* complaint history
There’s a strong perception difference between “things occasionally break” and “management ignores problems”
* If things occasionally break and they’re quickly addressed by management, tenants are unlikely to report a complaint. Complaints and violations likely indicate a lack of trust in landlords to address issues. 
A well-maintained older building can feel significantly more trustworthy than a newer luxury building with unresolved issues.
Implication
NYC open data can help surface:
* complaint frequency
* recurring issue categories
* violation severity
* Violation resolution patterns over time
The opportunity is not just exposing violations, but helping renters understand:
* Are issues isolated or systemic?
* Are problems getting resolved?
* Is building management proactive or reactive?

2. Have other tenants had issues here?
Renters heavily rely on social proof because they assume current and former tenants know the reality of the building better than any listing does.
People actively search for:
* tenant reviews
* Reddit discussions
* HPD complaints
* DOB violations
* Google reviews
* lawsuits
* recurring complaints
Renters may be scared off by patterns of issues. 
Examples:
* one noise complaint = normal NYC living
* repeated mold complaints across units = major concern
* recurring heat/hot water complaints = operational issue
* consistent pest complaints = management concern
Implication
The platform should help renters quickly identify:
* recurring complaint themes
* building trends over time
* issue concentration
* unresolved recurring problems
Potential framing:
* “Most common complaints in this building”
* “Complaint trend over the past 3 years”
* “How this building compares to similar buildings nearby”
________________


3. How does this building compare to alternatives nearby?
Renters rarely evaluate a building in isolation.
They compare:
* rent
* commute
* neighborhood
* management quality
* building condition
* safety
* amenities
* maintenance responsiveness
A renter may tolerate:
* an older building
* fewer amenities
* smaller units
if:
* management is responsive
* complaints are low
* issues are resolved quickly
* the building feels stable and predictable
People care less about perfection and more about relative value.
Implication
Data becomes more useful when contextualized, since raw violations alone can be misleading. 
* older/larger buildings naturally generate more complaints
* some neighborhoods report complaints differently
* density affects totals
The product should normalize and contextualize:
* complaint rates relative to nearby buildings
* violation trends relative to building size
* borough/neighborhood benchmarks
Potential framing:
* “Fewer heat complaints than similar buildings nearby”
* “Higher-than-average pest complaints for this area”
* “Complaint volume trending down year-over-year”
________________


4. Does this building have ongoing unresolved issues?
Prospective tenants distinguish between occasional issues. vs.chronic operational failures
The highest-concern categories appear to be:
* pests
* mold
* water damage
* heat/hot water
* elevator outages
* safety/security
* package theft
* persistent construction noise
The underlying concern is future stress and unpredictability.
Tenants want confidence that:
* problems get fixed
* issues don’t recur endlessly
* management is paying attention
Implication
The platform should prioritize trend visibility over static snapshots.
Potentially useful signals:
* repeated complaints in same category
* recurring complaints after resolution
* time-to-resolution estimates
* unresolved complaint accumulation
* year-over-year trend direction
This feels more actionable than simply displaying a long list of violations.

Risks / Things to Be Careful About
Raw violation counts can be misleading
Larger buildings naturally produce more complaints.
Normalization matters.
Complaints are not always verified; Violations are verified
Some complaints may be duplicates or unsubstantiated.
Important to distinguish:
* complaints
* violations
* resolved violations
Avoid fear-based UX
The goal should be informed decision-making, not panic.
The product feels strongest when framed around:
* transparency
* context
* comparability
* trend interpretation

Overall Takeaway
Prospective renters are not looking for perfect buildings.
They are looking for:
* predictability
* responsiveness
* transparency
* confidence that problems won’t spiral into daily stress


Appendix: Normalization approach:
The normalization approach is designed to make complaint comparisons fair across buildings of different sizes. Since raw complaint counts naturally skew higher for larger buildings, the system estimates building scale using footprint area and roof height as a proxy for gross building area. Estimated scale is calculated as footprint area multiplied by estimated floors, where estimated floors are derived from roof height divided by an assumed average floor height (~12 ft/floor). Complaint density is then computed by dividing weighted complaints by estimated building scale. 
Complaints can also be weighted by recency and severity so that recent or high-impact issues contribute more strongly than older or lower-severity complaints. Once complaint density is calculated, buildings are compared against similar-sized peer buildings rather than across all NYC buildings equally. The final score is percentile-based and converted into a 0–100 scale, where higher scores indicate fewer complaints relative to comparable buildings. This creates a more interpretable and trustworthy comparison system while avoiding unfairly penalizing large residential buildings.
